Over €100 a month on AI, and still more work?

I explain why Claude and Grok don't take bureaucracy off my hands, but mostly keep the user busy instead.

"I'll drop that into the chat for you as soon as we've finished talking."

That is what my AI agent says while I talk to it like an employee on the phone. It sounds like the future, but later I open the chat and it is empty. I ask whether something isn't missing. What comes back is only half of it.

Welcome to my everyday life with AI agents. For a few months now I have been using agentic AI intensively in my daily routine. By now I spend well over €100 a month on it in total.

My expectation: AI against red tape

Both privately and at work I deal with all kinds of tasks. A large part of my time does not go into technology but into documentation, logs and evidence. In other words, into the bureaucratic yoke that has been imposed on us Europeans more and more, and still is.

My hope was simple: that AI could take this off my hands. Keeping logs, maintaining documents, automating processes. I imagined taming the bureaucratic beast with it, maybe even partly throwing it off. But unfortunately things turned out a little differently.

Claude is brilliant, but feels like a civil servant

To be fair, the positive side first, because Claude is strong. For programming, research and drafting e-mails and documents it does save me time, and it often surprises me with ideas I would not have come up with myself. I have taught the AI workflows, set up jobs and built my own skills that it uses to get tasks done the way I want.

And that is exactly where the problem starts. It does help to formalise tasks to some extent, but if you have to describe everything in detail beforehand, the effort barely stays below what you wanted to achieve in the first place. Sometimes something works, and sometimes it works so well that you trust the AI to work more autonomously, and then it is the exact opposite again. That is very hard to predict.

Even more frustrating are the moments when Claude understands me very well but still does not do what I say. For example:

I keep a log in which something has to be done at 72-hour intervals. If I have exceeded the interval, Claude is supposed to split the period so that it matches the 72-hour interval. Any employee could do that, but Claude replies: "Unfortunately I can't do that."

To Claude this smells like backdating, and it suspects non-compliant behaviour.

I am not sure whether EU requirements or self-imposed rules are behind this. Either way, it is tedious to first have to convince your AI that you are not up to anything illegal. Here the AI slows you down as a user and even becomes the extended arm of the authorities, with access to potentially sensitive data.

Why I gave Grok a chance

Elon Musk strikes me as someone who would happily pick a fight with the authorities if it benefits the users. So I gave GrokBot a chance.

The solution promises that every agent operates its own computer in the cloud and therefore works even more autonomously, even when my laptop is offline. So-called always-on agents can react to events, for example when an e-mail lands in the inbox.

And for me that is a major strength of AI. AI should be able to hand out tasks while I am driving or on the road. For around €30 a month Grok was included in a subscription. That is a fair price for a digital employee, and here I would get as many as I like.

The scene from the beginning taught me otherwise. The handover from the conversation to the chat does not work well. What is promised "on the phone" arrives only halfway or not at all. On top of that you are tied to certain operating systems, for example Linux. The agent frequently gives up and does not try on its own to tackle a problem from different angles. Often you have to step in yourself and control the screen, for example to complete a login. The number of connectors is much more limited than with Claude, or at least the agent thinks so at first, and it does not suggest installing a connector even though that would be possible in theory. The digital employee needs me constantly and hardly works autonomously at all.

There is a bright spot after all

One area worked surprisingly well: checking and sending e-mails by voice works excellently with GrokBot. I use Claude to draft the e-mails, which I then review with GrokBot's voice assistant, improve where needed and have sent automatically. In these moments AI feels like a real assistant and genuinely takes work off my hands while raising the quality.

I cancelled anyway

Despite the bright spot, I cancelled Grok. I now pay well over €100 a month for AI that takes nowhere near as much work off my hands as I expected. Including the time I spend on it, it does not pay off for me (yet). But who knows, maybe in a few months or weeks that will be different again.

My conclusion: what AI can really do today

AI already helps me excellently with programming, research, drafts and small artefacts. Where it is well integrated, as with Excel or my e-mail workflow, it saves time and is pleasant to use.

A digital employee that works through bureaucracy on its own is not yet possible. For important matters, and that includes some programming tasks, it still pays off for me to do the work myself.

For it to pay off for me, two things have to change: agents have to work autonomously and more reliably, without asking back, and AI has to trust users to judge the task they set themselves and follow it, instead of always doubting and objecting.

Until then the bureaucratic beast stays for now, but maybe there is another way to outsmart it.
